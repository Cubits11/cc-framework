Guardrail stacks are commonly evaluated one component at a time. A report may
state that one guardrail fails on five percent of harmful prompts and another
fails on eight percent, then imply that their composition is safer than either
component alone. That implication depends on the unobserved joint distribution
of failures. If the same prompts defeat both guardrails, a stack can be much
less protective than an independence calculation suggests.

This paper studies the finite binary version of that problem. Each guardrail has
a failure indicator, and the composition question is encoded as a Boolean event
over those indicators. The central estimand is not a single score. It is the
identified interval of event probabilities compatible with the declared
evidence.

The contribution is intentionally narrow. We do not introduce new copula theory,
new partial-identification theory, or a deployment-safety certificate. We adapt
classical coupling and partial-identification machinery to composed AI
guardrail evaluation, define a reproducible artifact discipline around endpoint
witnesses, and show how an independence baseline can be reported as an
assumption-sensitive diagnostic rather than a default truth model.

The most important negative result is practical: sharp bounds can be wide. A
wide interval is still useful when it shows that singleton evidence is
insufficient for a claimed composition guarantee. The interval tells a reviewer
which additional dependence evidence would be needed to make the conclusion
decisive.
